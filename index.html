After an earthquake: the first hour

Southern Utah sits on active faults, including the Hurricane Fault. A strong earthquake is the emergency most likely to affect all of us at once. Phones, power, and roads may be down. Here is what we will each do.

  1. Drop, cover, and hold on until the shaking stops. Expect aftershocks.
  2. Check your household for injuries. Give first aid where you can.
  3. Check for hazards. Turn off the gas only if you smell gas or hear hissing. Watch for downed power lines and broken glass.
  4. Put your sign in the front window so neighbors and your ward contact can see it from the street:
    OKHELP
  5. Check on the neighbors around you, especially anyone who lives alone or has limited mobility.
  6. Report to your ward contact or come to the gathering point (below). Text instead of calling; texts get through when calls don't.
  7. Listen for official updates on a battery or crank radio: KDXU 890 AM, the area's main emergency alert station, or NOAA Weather Radio on 162.475 MHz.

Call 911 only for life-threatening emergencies so lines stay open for the people who need them most.

Other emergencies

Wildfire or evacuation

Keep a go-bag by the door with medications, documents, chargers, and a change of clothes. Leave as soon as you're told to; don't wait. Let your ward contact know where you went.

Extreme heat or summer power outage

A summer outage can become dangerous within hours. Know where you'd go to stay cool, keep extra water on hand, and check on older neighbors and anyone who relies on powered medical equipment. Never run a generator indoors or in the garage.

Flash flood

Never drive or walk through flowing water, even if it looks shallow. Stay out of washes and low crossings during and after storms, including storms miles away.

Water outage

Store at least one gallon per person per day for two weeks. Your water heater tank holds drinkable water too (turn off its gas or power before draining it).

If the income stops

For most families, a job loss or a large medical bill is far more likely than an earthquake, and 911 can't help with either. You don't have to do all of this at once. Each step makes a hard season easier.

Less debt

Fewer payments to cover when the paychecks stop.

First step: pay off your smallest debt.

Lower monthly costs

The less your household needs each month, the longer your money lasts.

First step: lower one monthly bill.

Savings

Savings buy you time to find the right next job.

First step: save one month of basic expenses, a little at a time.

A second way to earn

Jobs change, and some are changing quickly. A second skill gives you somewhere else to turn.

First step: take a class, earn a certificate, learn a trade, or try some side work.

If it happens: the first week

  • Talk to the bishop early. There's no shame in it, and it stays confidential.
  • Apply for unemployment benefits and list the bills that must be paid.
  • Eat from your pantry. That's what it's for, and every meal from it is money you don't spend.
  • Tell people you're looking. Many jobs come through someone you know.

Can you review a résumé, practice interviews, or pass along job leads? Add it under “Other” in the skills section of the Skills & Needs form.

Money and documents in a disaster

Losing income is one problem. A power outage or a flood is a different one, and it needs different preparation.

Cash that works when the systems don't

In a power outage, card readers, ATMs and the networks behind them all fail together. Stores that stay open go cash-only, and nobody can make change for large bills.

Keep some cash at home in small denominations — ones, fives and tens. Enough for a few days of fuel, food and incidentals. Keep it somewhere you could still reach if you had to leave in a hurry.

The insurance gap most households here have

Worth a phone call this week

Standard homeowner's and renter's policies do not cover flood damage. Not from a flooded wash, not from runoff, not from a storm drain backing up. Flood coverage is a separate policy, and it typically takes about 30 days to take effect — which means it cannot be bought once a storm is already in the forecast.

Because our hazard here is flash flooding rather than river flooding, homes near washes and drainages carry real exposure even outside mapped flood zones. Ask your agent one direct question: what happens if water enters this house from outside?

Two other reviews worth doing once a year: whether your dwelling coverage would actually rebuild at today's construction costs, and whether your contents are covered at replacement cost or at depreciated value.

Documents you cannot quickly replace

The practical standard is that you could reconstruct your financial life from your phone. Photograph or scan these, then keep them in two places — one physical copy somewhere safe, one encrypted digital copy you can reach from anywhere.

  • Birth certificates, Social Security cards, passports, driver's licenses
  • Deed or mortgage, vehicle titles, lease agreements
  • Insurance policies, with policy numbers and your agent's direct line
  • A prescription list with dosages, and immunization records
  • Bank and investment account numbers, with one recent statement from each
  • A room-by-room video walkthrough of the house, for a contents claim
  • Marriage certificate, wills, powers of attorney, advance directives

An afternoon with a phone camera covers most of this. If you do nothing else here, do that.

Building a longer-term food supply

The calculator tells you how much. This is what to buy, what quietly expires, and the equipment people forget until the day they need it.

Work in calories per bucket

Calorie figures get published per ounce, per cup, per 100 grams and per pound depending on where you look. Mixing those units is the most common mistake in a storage spreadsheet, and families usually end up believing they have far more or far less than they do. Checking against a per-container figure catches it fast.

A year at 2,000 calories a day is about 730,000 calories per person. Plan closer to 2,500 if you expect to be doing physical work without power, and it rises to roughly 912,500 — a difference of about three months of food. Decide which you are planning against before you start buying.

A plan is only as long as its shortest item

Thirty-year wheat stored beside one-year nuts is not a one-year supply. Part of it expires quietly while nobody is looking.

25 to 30 years

Wheat berries, white rice, dry beans, lentils, sugar, salt. Honey keeps indefinitely. Sealed in food-grade buckets with oxygen absorbers.

10 to 20 years

Rolled oats, powdered milk, powdered egg, freeze-dried fruit and vegetables. Better in #10 cans than buckets.

1 to 2 years

Nuts, nut butters, masa harina, whole-grain flour, brown rice, cooking oils. The oils in these go rancid. Freezer storage roughly doubles the window.

Under a year

Protein powders, baking powder, fresh-milled flour, most supplements. Rotation items, not storage items.

The ten-year bean problem

Dry beans stored past roughly a decade develop what is called hard-to-cook syndrome. They never soften no matter how long you boil them, and how well they were sealed makes no difference. Lentils and split peas do not do this. If beans are a large share of your plan, either rotate them or move some of that weight to lentils.

The things nobody budgets for

Food in a bucket is not food on a plate. Each of these turns stored staples into something you can actually eat.

  • A hand grain mill. Wheat berries without one are birdseed. Buy manual or manual-capable — an electric-only mill is a bet on the grid staying up.
  • Cooking fuel. Dry beans need one to two hours of boiling every time. Over months that is hundreds of hours of burner time. A pressure cooker cuts bean cooking by about two-thirds and is the cheapest fuel savings available.
  • Animal feed. Ten laying hens eat roughly 900 pounds of feed a year — more tonnage than most families' entire food storage, and it buys eggs rather than calories. Keep chickens for the protein and the morale, but do not count them as a calorie source.
  • Garden inputs. Seed that stays viable, and nutrient solution for any hydroponic setup. Without the consumables, a grow tower is a planter.
  • Salt, leavening and seasoning. Bread needs salt. And appetite fatigue is real — people stop eating monotonous food, so a plan nobody will eat is not a plan.

Water is the harder problem

Two weeks for a family of four is 56 gallons, which is easy and covers the most likely emergency by a wide margin. A year for one person is 365 gallons — about seven 55-gallon drums for a single adult. Past roughly a month, storage stops being the answer and filtration plus a renewable source becomes the answer. Budget for a gravity filter and spare elements alongside the containers.

In this climate, add one more thing: keep stored water out of the heat. A garage that hits 130°F in July degrades plastic containers faster than most people expect.

One household's worked example

A member of the ward built a full one-year plan for one adult using the method above — corrected calorie math, shelf-life tiers, home production counted honestly, and a weekly meal plan that reconciles against the stored quantities. Ask at church if you would like to see it.

It is an example of the arithmetic, not a plan to copy. It is built around one person's body weight, activity level, allergies and tastes, and its nutrient targets and supplement list would be wrong — in some cases meaningfully wrong — for a child, for a pregnant or nursing mother, or for anyone managing a health condition. Emotional resilience

Households rarely come apart in an emergency because they ran out of supplies. They struggle because of what sustained stress does to people, and to the relationships between them.

What stress actually does

Under prolonged strain most people experience some mix of disrupted sleep, a shorter fuse, trouble concentrating, physical symptoms with no obvious cause, and either withdrawal or a restless inability to settle. Children often show it as regression — behavior they had outgrown coming back — or as stomachaches and headaches rather than anything they can put a name to.

These are ordinary responses to hard circumstances. They are not character failures, and they are not evidence that someone is handling things badly. Knowing that ahead of time matters, because a family expecting the irritability is far less likely to read it as a sign that something is wrong between them.

What helps

Keep the structure

Meals at roughly the same times, consistent bedtimes, Sunday still being Sunday. Routine does more for a household under stress than nearly anything else, and it costs nothing.

Give people jobs

A role counters helplessness directly — including for children, with real tasks scaled to their age. Idle worry is corrosive.

Limit the intake

Check for information you can act on, then stop. Continuous coverage of something you cannot change raises distress without improving any decision.

Protect sleep first

Nearly everything else gets harder without it, and it is usually the first thing sacrificed.

Stay in contact

Isolation makes everything worse and is easy to drift into when everyone is stretched. A short conversation with someone outside the household is not a luxury.

Get outside and move

Even briefly. Early morning, in this climate.

Talking with children

Children judge how serious things are mostly from the adults around them rather than from the facts themselves.

  • Tell the truth, at the level of detail their age can hold. Vague reassurance that does not match what they can plainly see teaches them not to trust what you say.
  • Say what is being done, and who is doing it. Children settle on knowing that capable adults are handling it far more than on details.
  • Answer the question they asked, then stop. They will ask for more when they are ready for more.
  • Expect the same questions again and again. Repetition is how children process, not a sign they did not hear you.
  • Keep them away from continuous coverage, especially footage of damage.

What a ward is actually for

The practical function of a congregation in a long emergency is help that nobody has to ask for twice: knowing who lives alone, who has medical equipment that needs power, who cannot drive, who has a generator, who has a truck, who has the skills.

That knowledge has to exist before it is needed, which is the entire point of the skills and needs form. The households that do best are generally not the ones with the deepest storage. They are the ones embedded in a network of people who notice when they go quiet.

When it is more than ordinary stress

Some things warrant help beyond what family and friends can give: distress that does not ease as circumstances improve, being unable to function in daily responsibilities over a period of weeks, leaning on alcohol or other substances to get through the day, or thoughts of self-harm.

Reaching out for that is not a failure of faith, resilience or preparation. It is the same kind of decision as setting a broken bone.

If you or someone you know needs help now

The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is available 24 hours a day, every day. Call or text 988. It is free and confidential, and it is for anyone in distress — not only people in immediate danger.

The bishop can also connect members with counseling resources through Church channels, including help with the cost where that is a barrier.
