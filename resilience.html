Emotional resilience

Households rarely come apart in an emergency because they ran out of supplies. They struggle because of what sustained stress does to people, and to the relationships between them.

What stress actually does

Under prolonged strain most people experience some mix of disrupted sleep, a shorter fuse, trouble concentrating, physical symptoms with no obvious cause, and either withdrawal or a restless inability to settle. Children often show it as regression — behavior they had outgrown coming back — or as stomachaches and headaches rather than anything they can put a name to.

These are ordinary responses to hard circumstances. They are not character failures, and they are not evidence that someone is handling things badly. Knowing that ahead of time matters, because a family expecting the irritability is far less likely to read it as a sign that something is wrong between them.

What helps

Keep the structure

Meals at roughly the same times, consistent bedtimes, Sunday still being Sunday. Routine does more for a household under stress than nearly anything else, and it costs nothing.

Give people jobs

A role counters helplessness directly — including for children, with real tasks scaled to their age. Idle worry is corrosive.

Limit the intake

Check for information you can act on, then stop. Continuous coverage of something you cannot change raises distress without improving any decision.

Protect sleep first

Nearly everything else gets harder without it, and it is usually the first thing sacrificed.

Stay in contact

Isolation makes everything worse and is easy to drift into when everyone is stretched. A short conversation with someone outside the household is not a luxury.

Get outside and move

Even briefly. Early morning, in this climate.

Talking with children

Children judge how serious things are mostly from the adults around them rather than from the facts themselves.

  • Tell the truth, at the level of detail their age can hold. Vague reassurance that does not match what they can plainly see teaches them not to trust what you say.
  • Say what is being done, and who is doing it. Children settle on knowing that capable adults are handling it far more than on details.
  • Answer the question they asked, then stop. They will ask for more when they are ready for more.
  • Expect the same questions again and again. Repetition is how children process, not a sign they did not hear you.
  • Keep them away from continuous coverage, especially footage of damage.

What a ward is actually for

The practical function of a congregation in a long emergency is help that nobody has to ask for twice: knowing who lives alone, who has medical equipment that needs power, who cannot drive, who has a generator, who has a truck, who has the skills.

That knowledge has to exist before it is needed, which is the entire point of the skills and needs form. The households that do best are generally not the ones with the deepest storage. They are the ones embedded in a network of people who notice when they go quiet.

When it is more than ordinary stress

Some things warrant help beyond what family and friends can give: distress that does not ease as circumstances improve, being unable to function in daily responsibilities over a period of weeks, leaning on alcohol or other substances to get through the day, or thoughts of self-harm.

Reaching out for that is not a failure of faith, resilience or preparation. It is the same kind of decision as setting a broken bone.

If you or someone you know needs help now

The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is available 24 hours a day, every day. Call or text 988. It is free and confidential, and it is for anyone in distress — not only people in immediate danger.

The bishop can also connect members with counseling resources through Church channels, including help with the cost where that is a barrier.
