What we are asked to store

Two different numbers circulate among members, and both are real counsel. Knowing which one is current saves a good deal of confusion — and a good deal of discouragement.

The counsel today

The Church's published guidance is All Is Safely Gathered In: Family Home Storage, and it asks for four things rather than a single number:

  1. A three-month supply of the food you normally eat.
  2. Drinking water, for times when the supply may be polluted or cut off.
  3. A financial reserve, built a little at a time.
  4. A longer-term supply of basics such as wheat, rice, beans and oats — where permitted, and as circumstances allow.

Build a small supply of food that is part of your normal, daily diet until it is sufficient for three months.

For longer-term needs, and where permitted, gradually build a supply with food that will last a long time.

All Is Safely Gathered In: Family Home Storage (2007)

Notice what is not there: the longer-term supply has no stated quantity. The same pamphlet asks members not to go to extremes or into debt to build storage quickly.

Where the year’s supply comes from

The one-year figure is real and it is old. President J. Reuben Clark Jr., then a counselor in the First Presidency, said in April 1937 general conference:

Let every head of every household see to it that he has on hand enough food and clothing, and, where possible, fuel also, for at least a year ahead.

J. Reuben Clark Jr., Conference Report, April 1937

It was repeated for decades. The version most members remember is President Ezra Taft Benson in October 1980, and it carries the qualifier people often recall:

… store a provision of food which will last for at least a year wherever it is legally permissible to do so.

Ezra Taft Benson, “Prepare for the Days of Tribulation,” Ensign, November 1980

So which one applies?

Three things are true at once, and they are easy to run together:

  • The legal qualifier survived. Current materials still say “where permitted,” and the pamphlet's First Presidency message notes that some members “may be prohibited by law from storing large amounts of food.”
  • The one-year number did not carry into current materials. It is not in the pamphlet, the Gospel Topics page, or the official FAQ.
  • The earlier counsel was never repudiated. The 2007 change was presented as making storage achievable for members everywhere, not as a correction.

The practical summary

Three months is what we are asked for today. A year is historical counsel that many members still work toward, and there is nothing wrong with doing so where you have the space, the means and the legal latitude.

If you are starting from nothing, start with three months of what you already eat. That is the counsel, and it is also the part that will actually get done.

Longer-term basics

These are common planning estimates, not official requirements. Children are counted as 70% of an adult and little ones as 50%. Water is 1 gallon per person per day for drinking and basic hygiene, pets need their own. Start where you can: a little at a time is how every family's supply gets built.

Building a longer-term food supply

The calculator tells you how much. This is what to buy, what quietly expires, and the equipment people forget until the day they need it.

Work in calories per bucket

Calorie figures get published per ounce, per cup, per 100 grams and per pound depending on where you look. Mixing those units is the most common mistake in a storage spreadsheet, and families usually end up believing they have far more or far less than they do. Checking against a per-container figure catches it fast.

A year at 2,000 calories a day is about 730,000 calories per person. Plan closer to 2,500 if you expect to be doing physical work without power, and it rises to roughly 912,500 — a difference of about three months of food. Decide which you are planning against before you start buying.

A plan is only as long as its shortest item

Thirty-year wheat stored beside one-year nuts is not a one-year supply. Part of it expires quietly while nobody is looking.

25 to 30 years

Wheat berries, white rice, dry beans, lentils, sugar, salt. Honey keeps indefinitely. Sealed in food-grade buckets with oxygen absorbers.

10 to 20 years

Rolled oats, powdered milk, powdered egg, freeze-dried fruit and vegetables. Better in #10 cans than buckets.

1 to 2 years

Nuts, nut butters, masa harina, whole-grain flour, brown rice, cooking oils. The oils in these go rancid. Freezer storage roughly doubles the window.

Under a year

Protein powders, baking powder, fresh-milled flour, most supplements. Rotation items, not storage items.

The ten-year bean problem

Dry beans stored past roughly a decade develop what is called hard-to-cook syndrome. They never soften no matter how long you boil them, and how well they were sealed makes no difference. Lentils and split peas do not do this. If beans are a large share of your plan, either rotate them or move some of that weight to lentils.

The things nobody budgets for

Food in a bucket is not food on a plate. Each of these turns stored staples into something you can actually eat.

  • A hand grain mill. Wheat berries without one are birdseed. Buy manual or manual-capable — an electric-only mill is a bet on the grid staying up.
  • Cooking fuel. Dry beans need one to two hours of boiling every time. Over months that is hundreds of hours of burner time. A pressure cooker cuts bean cooking by about two-thirds and is the cheapest fuel savings available.
  • Animal feed. Ten laying hens eat roughly 900 pounds of feed a year — more tonnage than most families' entire food storage, and it buys eggs rather than calories. Keep chickens for the protein and the morale, but do not count them as a calorie source.
  • Garden inputs. Seed that stays viable, and nutrient solution for any hydroponic setup. Without the consumables, a grow tower is a planter.
  • Salt, leavening and seasoning. Bread needs salt. And appetite fatigue is real — people stop eating monotonous food, so a plan nobody will eat is not a plan.

Water is the harder problem

Two weeks for a family of four is 56 gallons, which is easy and covers the most likely emergency by a wide margin. A year for one person is 365 gallons — about seven 55-gallon drums for a single adult. Past roughly a month, storage stops being the answer and filtration plus a renewable source becomes the answer. Budget for a gravity filter and spare elements alongside the containers.

In this climate, add one more thing: keep stored water out of the heat. A garage that hits 130°F in July degrades plastic containers faster than most people expect.

One household's worked example

A member of the ward built a full one-year plan for one adult using the method above — corrected calorie math, shelf-life tiers, home production counted honestly, and a weekly meal plan that reconciles against the stored quantities. Ask at church if you would like to see it.

It is an example of the arithmetic, not a plan to copy. It is built around one person's body weight, activity level, allergies and tastes, and its nutrient targets and supplement list would be wrong — in some cases meaningfully wrong — for a child, for a pregnant or nursing mother, or for anyone managing a health condition. Borrow the structure and build your own numbers.
