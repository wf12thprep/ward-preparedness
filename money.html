If the income stops

For most families, a job loss or a large medical bill is far more likely than an earthquake, and 911 can't help with either. You don't have to do all of this at once. Each step makes a hard season easier.

Less debt

Fewer payments to cover when the paychecks stop.

First step: pay off your smallest debt.

Lower monthly costs

The less your household needs each month, the longer your money lasts.

First step: lower one monthly bill.

Savings

Savings buy you time to find the right next job.

First step: save one month of basic expenses, a little at a time.

A second way to earn

Jobs change, and some are changing quickly. A second skill gives you somewhere else to turn.

First step: take a class, earn a certificate, learn a trade, or try some side work.

If it happens: the first week

  • Talk to the bishop early. There's no shame in it, and it stays confidential.
  • Apply for unemployment benefits and list the bills that must be paid.
  • Eat from your pantry. That's what it's for, and every meal from it is money you don't spend.
  • Tell people you're looking. Many jobs come through someone you know.

Can you review a résumé, practice interviews, or pass along job leads? Add it under “Other” in the skills section of the Skills & Needs form.

Money and documents in a disaster

Losing income is one problem. A power outage or a flood is a different one, and it needs different preparation.

Cash that works when the systems don't

In a power outage, card readers, ATMs and the networks behind them all fail together. Stores that stay open go cash-only, and nobody can make change for large bills.

Keep some cash at home in small denominations — ones, fives and tens. Enough for a few days of fuel, food and incidentals. Keep it somewhere you could still reach if you had to leave in a hurry.

The insurance gap most households here have

Worth a phone call this week

Standard homeowner's and renter's policies do not cover flood damage. Not from a flooded wash, not from runoff, not from a storm drain backing up. Flood coverage is a separate policy, and it typically takes about 30 days to take effect — which means it cannot be bought once a storm is already in the forecast.

Because our hazard here is flash flooding rather than river flooding, homes near washes and drainages carry real exposure even outside mapped flood zones. Ask your agent one direct question: what happens if water enters this house from outside?

Two other reviews worth doing once a year: whether your dwelling coverage would actually rebuild at today's construction costs, and whether your contents are covered at replacement cost or at depreciated value.

Documents you cannot quickly replace

The practical standard is that you could reconstruct your financial life from your phone. Photograph or scan these, then keep them in two places — one physical copy somewhere safe, one encrypted digital copy you can reach from anywhere.

  • Birth certificates, Social Security cards, passports, driver's licenses
  • Deed or mortgage, vehicle titles, lease agreements
  • Insurance policies, with policy numbers and your agent's direct line
  • A prescription list with dosages, and immunization records
  • Bank and investment account numbers, with one recent statement from each
  • A room-by-room video walkthrough of the house, for a contents claim
  • Marriage certificate, wills, powers of attorney, advance directives

An afternoon with a phone camera covers most of this. If you do nothing else here, do that.
